Quorum sensing

Alternatives to classical antibiotics are considered a promising strategy to control the spread of antimicrobial resistance. Among them, interference with bacterial quorum sensing (QS) has received particular attention. QS is a cell-to-cell communication system mediated by chemical signals called autoinducers (AIs). In Gram-negative bacteria, conserved QS pathways rely on acyl-homoserine lactones (AHLs), which are produced by LuxI-type synthases and detected by LuxR-type regulators. QS plays a key role in bacterial adaptation to stress and is strongly involved in virulence and pathogenesis.

In the AVS Lab, quorum sensing research was established through a long-standing scientific collaboration with Celina Vila Sanjurjo, PhD, whose background integrates nanotechnology, pharmaceutical sciences, molecular biology, and microbiology, with particular expertise in nanotechnologies for drug delivery and bacterial modulation.

Main research lines

1. QS modelling

We develop and use synthetic bacterial QS biosensors as quantitative platforms to evaluate anti-QS compounds. In particular, we employ agent-based modelling to simulate the QS response of an engineered Escherichia coli biosensor carrying the Aliivibrio fischeri LuxR/LuxI system coupled to a fluorescent reporter.

We have shown that percolation theory can describe QS activation dynamics both in vivo and in silico, providing a useful mathematical framework to interpret the action of QS inhibitors. Within this context, cinnamaldehyde (CA) was shown to delay QS activation by increasing the apparent percolation critical time. Computational simulations support a mechanism in which CA inactivates LuxR in its AHL-bound form, leading to a delayed signalling response.

In parallel, we investigate how percolation-like processes shape bacterial population behaviour using the Cayley Tree model integrated into a computational framework. These analyses suggest that QS responses can shift between stochastic and deterministic regimes depending on LuxR levels, highlighting the role of molecular fluctuations in collective bacterial behaviour.

References: PhD Thesis (Celina Vila Sanjurjo); TFM (Daniel Bello Álvarez); TFG/TFM (Alejandro Novoa Pello).

2. Deeper characterization of QS in Chromobacterium violaceum

The production of the violet pigment violacein in Chromobacterium violaceum is regulated by QS. Building on previous work using the CV026 mutant strain, derived from C. violaceum ATCC31532, we study new QS-deficient mutants to further dissect the regulatory network controlling violacein production.

We combine growth analysis, scanning electron microscopy, and fluorescent reporters linked to QS genes to monitor gene expression and pigment-associated processes. Our results suggest that the current model of violacein regulation needs revision. In particular, expression driven by the vioA promoter appears to be constitutive, indicating that QS regulation may act at a different level.

Additional observations include QS-dependent fluorescence with spectral features compatible with violacein production, as well as filamentous structures potentially involved in biofilm formation and cell aggregation.

Ongoing work focuses on the proteomic characterization of C. violaceum mutants under inducing and non-inducing conditions to identify the molecular mechanisms underlying QS regulation and biofilm-associated phenotypes.
