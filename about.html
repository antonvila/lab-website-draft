About

Antón Vila‑Sanjurjo is Associate Professor of Genetics at the Universidade da Coruña (UDC), Spain.

Biographical Sketch

My scientific career has followed an unconventional path shaped by interdisciplinary training, international experience, and a long-standing fascination with one of the most fundamental questions in biology: how genetic information is translated into phenotype.

I began my academic studies in Business Administration at the University of Santiago de Compostela (Spain), before discovering a passion for biology and transferring to the University of A Coruña (UDC). While still an undergraduate student, I joined the Biotechnology Program at the University of Nebraska at Omaha (USA), where I was first introduced to the study of the ribosome. Work developed during those years under the supervision of Dr. William E. Tapprich culminated in my first scientific publication in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS), an event that profoundly influenced the direction of my future scientific career.

My doctoral training took place at Brown University (Providence, Rhode Island, USA) under the mentorship of Professor Albert E. Dahlberg, one of the pioneers of modern ribosome research. Under his guidance, I developed a deep interest in the structure, function, and fidelity of the ribosome. This work laid the foundation for what would become the central theme of my scientific journey: understanding how molecular mechanisms governing protein synthesis influence cellular physiology, adaptation, and disease.

Following my Ph.D., I joined the laboratory of Professor Jamie H. D. Cate at the Whitehead Institute for Biomedical Research (MIT) and later at the University of California, Berkeley. Working alongside Dr. Cate and an exceptional group of structural biologists, I participated in pioneering studies that revealed the three-dimensional architecture of the bacterial ribosome. These efforts resulted in a series of high-impact publications, including papers in Science, PNAS, Nature Structural Biology, Nature Structural & Molecular Biology, and Nucleic Acids Research. This work contributed significantly to our understanding of ribosome structure and function and was recognized through the Alfred Tissières Young Investigator Award from the International Society of Cellular Stress. More importantly, these years provided me with a rigorous foundation in structural biology and translational fidelity that continues to shape my research today.

After completing my postdoctoral work, I joined the laboratory of Professor Carlos Bustamante at the University of California, Berkeley. Together, we co-led an ambitious synthetic biology project aimed at designing a minimal living cell using the mitochondrion as a cellular chassis. This project exposed me to systems-level approaches to biological design and strengthened my interest in mitochondria as a unique evolutionary platform connecting genetics, bioenergetics, and cellular regulation. It also reinforced my conviction that major scientific advances often emerge at the intersection of traditionally separate disciplines.

In 2010, I had the privilege of receiving a Marie Curie International Incoming Fellowship from the European Union, which allowed me to return to the University of A Coruña and establish an independent research program. This marked the beginning of a new phase of my career in which I brought together my expertise in ribosome biology with a growing interest in mitochondrial genetics and disease.

Together with colleagues at the Wellcome Centre for Mitochondrial Research and Newcastle University, including Professors Robert W. Taylor, Robert N. Lightowlers, Zofia M. Chrzanowska-Lightowlers, Paul M. Smith, and Joanna L. Elson, I helped develop a new framework for the interpretation of pathogenic mitochondrial ribosomal RNA variants. This work led to the creation of Heterologous Inferential Analysis (HIA), a methodology that has provided novel insights into the contribution of mitochondrial rRNA mutations to human disease. Through this international collaborative effort, we established a productive research program that continues to generate important discoveries regarding mitochondrial dysfunction, translational fidelity, and human health.

A major influence in the development of my current ideas has been my collaboration with Professor John F. Atkins, whose pioneering work helped establish the modern study of translational fidelity. Our recent collaborations have contributed to refining hypotheses concerning mitochondrial translation accuracy and its role in disease, aging, and cellular adaptation.

Although my research interests have broadened over time, they remain connected by a common conceptual thread: understanding how information encoded in genetic systems is translated into biological function. This trajectory has taken me from bacterial ribosome structure to mitochondrial genetics, from translational fidelity to systems biology, and from basic molecular mechanisms to human disease.

Over the last decade, I have increasingly incorporated multi-omics approaches into my research. Through collaborations with the U.S. Naval Medical Research Unit No. 6 (NAMRU-6), Vanderbilt University, and the Pontificia Universidad Católica del Perú, I have participated in projects funded by the U.S. Department of Defense and the National Institutes of Health. These studies employed transcriptomics, ribosome profiling, and systems biology to investigate infectious diseases, vaccine responses, and host-pathogen interactions. This work resulted in the development of novel analytical approaches and publications in journals such as NPJ Vaccines and The Journal of Infectious Diseases.

At the same time, I have maintained a strong interest in technology development and knowledge transfer. My work has contributed to patented technologies involving translational analysis and vaccine development, while collaborations with industry and government agencies have expanded my research into aquaculture biotechnology, fish health, environmental bioremediation, and sustainable food production. More recently, projects such as MIVALI and BIOROCK have extended these efforts towards innovative approaches for aquatic animal health, environmental biotechnology, and sustainable aquaculture.

Today, my laboratory integrates structural biology, mitochondrial genetics, ribosome biology, multi-omics, systems biology, and biotechnology. Our current research focuses on understanding how mitochondrial translation fidelity, mitonuclear interactions, and mitochondrial genome variation influence human disease, adaptation, organismal fitness, and environmental sustainability.

Looking back, what may appear as a non-linear career trajectory has in fact been a continuous scientific exploration of a single overarching question: how genetic information shapes life across scales, from individual nucleotides to complex organisms.
